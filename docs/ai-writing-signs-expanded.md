# Signs of AI-Generated Writing on Wikipedia

This is a list of writing and formatting conventions typical of AI chatbots such as ChatGPT, with real examples taken from Wikipedia articles, drafts, comments, and other content. It is a field guide to help detect undisclosed AI-generated content on Wikipedia: while some of the signs may be broadly applicable, some may not apply in a non-Wikipedia context.[a] Not all text featuring these indicators is AI-generated, as the large language models that power AI chatbots are trained on human writing, including Wikipedia. Many elements of AI writing can be found in editorials, blogs, or fan fiction.

Moreover, this list is descriptive, not prescriptive; it consists of observations, not rules. Advice about formatting or language to avoid can be found in the policies and guidelines and the Manual of Style, but does not belong on this page.

The patterns listed here are also only potential signs of a problem, not the problem itself. While many of these issues are immediately obvious and easy to fix—e.g., excessive boldface, broken markup, citation style quirks—they can point to less outwardly visible problems that carry much more serious policy risks. Please do not merely treat these signs as the problems to be fixed; that could just make detection harder. The actual problems are those deeper concerns, so make sure to address them, either yourself or by flagging them, per the relevant guide.

The speedy deletion policy criterion G15 (LLM-generated pages without human review) lists some signs of AI writing, but is limited to the most objective ones. The remaining signs covered here are not sufficient on their own for speedy deletion.

## Caveats

### AI detection tools

> **Shortcut:** `WP:AIDETECTION`

Do not solely rely on artificial intelligence content detection tools (such as GPTZero). While they perform better than random chance, these tools have non-trivial error rates.[1][2] Detectors can be susceptible to factors such as text modifications (e.g. paraphrasing, markup, and spacing changes) and the use of models not seen during detector training.[3] A high percentage of detected AI writing from the results of a detection tool is not a valid criterion for speedy deletion under G15.

### Your detection ability

> **Shortcut:** `WP:AIDETECTIVE`

Test your AI detection skills at Wikipedia:AI or not quiz.

Do not rely too much on your own judgment. Humans are notoriously bad at distinguishing human and LLM-generated text. While research on humans' abilities to detect AI-generated text is still limited, a 2025 study has shown that human ability to distinguish LLM text from human is no better than random chance.[4] Another 2025 study on German theses has shown that humans managed a "recognition rate of 57 % for AI texts and 64 % for human-generated texts".[5]

A 2025 preprint has shown that heavy users of LLMs can correctly determine whether an article was generated by AI about 90% of the time, which means that if you are an expert user of LLMs and you tag 10 pages as being AI-generated, you've probably made one false positive. Study participants who didn't use LLMs much did only slightly better than random chance (in both directions).[1]

Note, also, that human speech and writing is being influenced by LLMs, and thus they are becoming more similar. This was already evident in 2024, as shown by a study that detected a significant LLM influence in spoken content (e.g. conversational podcasts).[6] Further studies seem to confirm this influence on language,[7] including semantics and word choices.[8]

It is also worth noting that writers may adjust their behavior to avoid accusations of AI, or may be defensive about using AI tropes.

## Content

> **Shortcuts:** `WP:AIWTW` · `WP:AI-ISM` · `WP:LLMISM`

LLMs (and artificial neural networks in general) use statistical algorithms to guess (infer) what should come next based on a large corpus of training material. It thus tends to regress to the mean; that is, the result tends toward the most statistically likely result that applies to the widest variety of cases. It can simultaneously be a strength and a "tell" for detecting AI-generated content.

For example, LLMs are usually trained on data from the internet in which famous people are generally described with positive, important-sounding language. Consequently, the LLM tends to omit specific, unusual, nuanced facts (which are statistically rare) and replace them with more generic, positive descriptions (which are statistically common). Thus the highly specific "inventor of the first train-coupling device" might become "a revolutionary titan of industry". It is like shouting louder and louder that a portrait shows a uniquely important person, while the portrait itself is fading from a sharp photograph into a blurry, generic sketch. The subject becomes simultaneously less specific and more exaggerated.[b]

This statistical regression to the mean, a smoothing over of specific facts into generic statements, that could equally apply to many topics, makes AI-generated content easier to detect.

### Undue emphasis on significance, legacy, and broader trends

> **Shortcuts:** `WP:AILEGACY` · `WP:AITREND`

> **Words to watch:** stands/serves as, is a testament/reminder, a vital/significant/crucial/pivotal/key role/moment, underscores/highlights its importance/significance, reflects broader, symbolizing its ongoing/enduring/lasting, contributing to the, setting the stage for, marking/shaping the, represents/marks a shift, key turning point, evolving landscape, focal point, indelible mark, deeply rooted,  ...

LLM writing often puffs up the importance of the subject matter by adding statements about how arbitrary aspects of the topic represent or contribute to a broader topic.[9][10] There is a distinct and easily identifiable repertoire of ways that it writes these statements.[11]

The Statistical Institute of Catalonia was officially established in 1989, marking a pivotal moment in the evolution of regional statistics in Spain. [...]

The founding of Idescat represented a significant shift toward regional statistical independence, enabling Catalonia to develop a statistical system tailored to its unique socio-economic context. This initiative was part of a broader movement across Spain to decentralize administrative functions and enhance regional governance.

> *— From this September 2024 revision to Statistical Institute of Catalonia*

Kumba has long been an important center for trade and agriculture. [...] The establishment of road networks connecting Kumba to other parts of the Southwest Region, such as Mamfe and Buea, helped solidify its role as a regional hub.

> *— From this October 2024 revision to Kumba, Cameroon*

The phenomenon has generated debate about authenticity, consent, and the psychological effects of digitally extending personhood.

[...]

Collectively, these works have shaped emerging policy discussions about ownership, consent, and dignity in digital resurrection technologies.

[...]

> *— From this October 2025 revision to Deadbot; each sentence here follows the rule of three, and the last one uses curly quotation marks*

LLMs may even include these statements for even the most mundane of subjects like etymology or population data. Sometimes, they add hedging preambles acknowledging that the subject is of relatively low importance, before talking about its importance anyway.

#### Examples

During the Spanish colonial period, the name Bakunutan was hispanized to Bacnotan, a modification reflected in official documents preserved in the National Archives in Manila. This etymology highlights the enduring legacy of the community's resistance and the transformative power of unity in shaping its identity.

> *— From this December 2024 revision to Bacnotan*

> *— From Draft:Goebel Goe II (July 2025)*

When talking about biology (e.g., when asked to discuss an animal or plant species), LLMs tend to over-emphasize connections to the broader ecosystem or environment, even when those connections are tenuous or generic. LLMs also tend to belabor the species' conservation status and research and preservation efforts, even if the status is unknown and no serious efforts exist.

#### Examples

However, the general health of the Lake Malawi ecosystem is crucial for the survival of this and other endemic species. Factors such as overfishing, pollution, and habitat destruction could potentially impact their populations.

> *— From this July 2024 revision to Lethrinops lethrinus*

> *— From this December 2024 revision to Nototrichium divaricatum*

### Canned emphasis on notability, attribution, and media coverage

> **Shortcuts:** `WP:OVERATTRIBUTION` · `WP:AIATTR`

> **Words to watch:** independent coverage, local/regional/national/[country name] media outlets, music/business/tech outlets, trade publications, profiled in, written by a leading expert, active social media presence

Similarly, LLMs act as if the best way to prove that a subject is notable is to hit readers over the head with claims of notability, often by listing sources that a subject has been covered in and specifying what kind of sources they are (e.g., trade publications, regional media, etc). They often inaccurately attribute their own superficial analyses to the source. This is more common in text from newer AI tools (2025 or later).

Human-written press releases have of course also cited news clippings for decades, but LLMs specifically asked to write a Wikipedia article often echo the exact wording of Wikipedia's guidelines, such as "independent coverage."

#### Examples

She spoke about AI on CNN, and was featured in Vogue, Wired, Toronto Star, and other media. [...] Her insights have also been featured in *Wired*, *Refinery29*, and other prominent media outlets.

> *— From this February 2025 revision to Sinead Bovell (also note the use of Markdown)*

On Wikipedia specifically, LLMs often painstakingly emphasize their sources in the body text—even for trivial coverage, uncontroversial facts, or other situations where a human Wikipedia editor would be more likely to either provide an inline citation or no source at all.

#### Examples

> *— Trivial coverage with attribution, from this August 2025 revision to The Original Pancake Kitchen; the reference added for this sentence did not exist.*

In articles about people or entities that use social media, LLMs will often note that they "maintain an active social media presence" or something similar. This wording is particularly idiosyncratic to AI text and relatively uncommon on Wikipedia before ~2024.

#### Examples

The mall maintains a strong digital presence, particularly on Instagram, where it actively shares the latest updates and events. Forum Kochi has consistently demonstrated excellence in digital promotions, with high-quality, engaging, and impactful video content playing a key role in its outreach.

> *— From this June 2025 revision to Forum Mall Kochi*

### Superficial analyses

> **Shortcut:** `WP:SUPERFICIAL`

> **Words to watch:** highlighting/underscoring/emphasizing ..., ensuring ..., reflecting/symbolizing ..., contributing to ..., cultivating/fostering ..., encompassing ..., enhancing ..., valuable insights, align/resonate with,

AI chatbots tend to insert superficial analysis of information, often in relation to its significance, recognition, or impact.[12] This is often done by attaching a present participle ("-ing") phrase at the end of sentences, sometimes with vague attributions to third parties (see below).[12][9]

For the purpose of Wikipedia, such comments are usually synthesis or unattributed opinions. Newer chatbots with retrieval-augmented generation (for example, an AI chatbot that can search the web) may attach these statements to named sources—e.g., "Roger Ebert highlighted the lasting influence"—regardless of whether those sources say anything close.

### Promotional and advertisement-like language

> For non-AI-specific guidance about this, see Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Words to watch § Puffery.

*See also: Wikipedia:Marketing buzzspeak § Marketing buzzspeak and artificial intelligence*

> **Shortcuts:** `WP:AIPUFFERY` · `WP:AIPEACOCK`

> **Words to watch:** boasts a, vibrant, rich, profound, enhancing, showcasing, exemplifies, commitment to, natural beauty, nestled, in the heart of, groundbreaking, renowned, featuring, diverse array,  ...

LLMs have serious problems keeping a neutral tone. Even when prompted to use an encyclopedic style, their output will often tend toward advertisement-like writing, or like the prose of a travel guide. This may happen when generating new text or rewriting existing text: for instance, an edit summary claiming a rewrite "removed promotional tone" while actually introducing it. This may also happen when editors are not deliberately trying to advertise a subject.[13]

> **Note:** Not all promotional or spammy writing is AI-generated. LLMs tend to over-use the same set of promotional phrases no matter what the topic. Also, older LLMs (e.g., GPT-4) tend to output more blatantly positive text[14] than newer LLMs, which are more subtly positive and tend to avoid obviously superlative statements like "the best."

### Vague attributions and overgeneralization of opinions

> For non-AI-specific guidance about this, see Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Words to watch § Unsupported attributions.

> **Shortcut:** `WP:AIWEASEL`

> **Words to watch:** Industry reports, Observers have cited, Experts argue, Some critics argue, several sources/publications (when only few sources are cited), such as (before exhaustive word lists),  ...

AI chatbots tend to attribute opinions or claims to some vague authority—a practice called weasel wording.

#### Examples

Due to its unique characteristics, the Haolai River is of interest to researchers and conservationists. Efforts are ongoing to monitor its ecological health and preserve the surrounding grassland environment, which is part of a larger initiative to protect China’s semi-arid ecosystems from degradation.

> *— From this June 2025 revision to Haolai River*

The Kwararafa (Kororofa) confederacy is described in scholarship as a shifting Benue valley coalition led by Jukun groups and incorporating a range of Middle Belt peoples. Because much of the historical record derives from Hausa chronicles, Bornu sources and oral tradition, modern researchers treat Kwararafa as a fluid political and cultural formation rather than a fixed state.

> *— From this November 2025 revision to Kwararafa Confederacy*

AI chatbots also commonly exaggerate the quantity of sources that these opinions are attributed to. They may present views from one or two sources as widely held (often combined with the vague attributions above), mention the existence or opinion of multiple "reviewers" or "scholars" while only citing one person, or imply that lists of examples are non-exhaustive when the sources give no indication that other examples exist.

### Outline-like conclusions about challenges and future prospects

> **Words to watch:** Despite its... faces several challenges..., Despite these challenges, Challenges and Legacy, Future Outlook ...

Many LLM-generated Wikipedia articles include a "Challenges" section, which typically begins with a sentence like "Despite its [positive/promotional words], [article subject] faces challenges..." and ends with either a vaguely positive assessment of the article subject,[1] or speculation about how ongoing or potential initiatives could benefit the subject. Such paragraphs usually appear at the end of articles with a rigid outline structure, which may also include a separate section for "Future Prospects."

> **Note:** This sign is about the rigid formula, not simply the mention of challenges or challenging.

#### Examples

Challenges and Future Directions

As the global economy continues to evolve, international economic law faces new challenges and opportunities. [...] The future of international economic law lies in its ability to adapt to these emerging trends and continue to facilitate a stable and equitable global economic order.

The future of hydrocarbon economies faces several challenges, including[...] This section would speculate on potential developments and the changing landscape of global energy.

### Leads treating Wikipedia lists or broad article titles as proper nouns

In AI-generated articles about topics with a title that is not a proper name, such as a list, the first sentence of the lead may introduce or define the article's title as if it were a standalone real-world entity. While the MOS does allow such titles to be included at the beginning of the lead "in a natural way", these AI leads tend not to be so natural.

#### Examples

Catchment area (health) refers to the geographic area from which a health facility, such as a hospital or clinic, draws its patients.

## Language and grammar

AI-generated text displays consistent patterns in syntax, word choice, and sentence construction that human writing does not display to nearly the same degree. Conversely, it often struggles to match some syntactic and linguistic patterns characteristic of human writing. Some LLMs deviate more from human writing than others; for example, GPT-4o, the language model used by ChatGPT from May 2024 to August 2025, produces output with more variation from human writing than other contemporaneous language models.[12] Since these are linguistic patterns, they occur consistently regardless of the subject matter, which often gives AI-generated text an identifiable "voice".

### High density of "AI vocabulary" words

> **Shortcuts:** `WP:AIVOCAB` · `WP:AIWORDS`

> **Words to watch:** Additionally (especially beginning a sentence),[15][16] align with,[11][17] boasts (meaning "has"),[18] bolstered,[17] crucial,[1][15][16] delve,[11][17][1][19] emphasizing,[11][17] enduring,[17] enhance,[17][1] fostering,[17][1] garner,[11][17] highlight (as a verb),[1][19] interplay,[17] intricate/intricacies,[11][17][12][19] key (as an adjective),[citation needed] landscape (as an abstract noun),[1] meticulous/meticulously,[18][17] pivotal,[17][1] robust, showcase,[11][17][19] tapestry (as an abstract noun),[1][12][19] testament,[1] underscore (as a verb),[11][17][1][19] valuable,[15] vibrant[12]

Many studies have demonstrated that LLMs overuse specific words. These words started appearing far more frequently in text produced after 2022, when LLM chatbots became widely accessible.[11][17] They often co-occur in LLM output: where there is one, there are likely others.[20] While most of these studies have analyzed scientific abstracts or fiction, "AI vocabulary" words are also ubiquitous in LLM-based encyclopedias, such as Grokipedia, and in AI-generated Wikipedia text. One or two of these words appearing in an edit may be coincidental, but an edit (post-2022) introducing lots of them, lots of times, is one of the strongest tells for AI use.

The words that LLMs overuse have changed over time. For instance, the word delve was famously overused by ChatGPT in 2023 and early 2024, but became less frequent later in 2024, then dropped off sharply in 2025.[21][15] Below is a breakdown of which words frequently recur together during which LLM "era". While these are not hard cutoffs, they should give you a rough idea of how "earlier" vs "later" LLM output reads.

2023 to mid-2024 (GPT-4): Additionally, boasts, bolstered, crucial, delve, emphasizing, enduring, garner, intricate/intricacies, interplay, key, landscape, meticulous/meticulously, pivotal, underscore, tapestry, testament, valuable, vibrant

Mid-2024 to mid-2025 (GPT-4o): align with, bolstered, crucial, emphasizing, enhance, enduring, fostering, highlighting, pivotal, showcasing, underscore, vibrant

Mid-2025 and on (GPT-5): emphasizing, enhance, highlighting, showcasing (plus words associated with "Undue emphasis on notability, attribution, and media coverage")

The distribution of "AI vocabulary" is also somewhat different depending on the chatbot or LLM used.[12] Grok output is particularly idiosyncratic: it overuses superficially "scientific" words like causal, empirical, correlate, and continues to overuse underscore as of 2026.

This section is to be taken as literally as possible: a word being overused by AI does not imply that its synonyms are also overused. Also, keep context in mind. For example, while the figurative use of "underscore" is ubiquitous in earlier AI text, the word can also refer to a literal underline mark or to incidental music.

### Avoidance of basic copulatives ("is"/"are" phrases)

> **Words to watch:** serves as/stands as/marks/represents [a], boasts/features/maintains/offers [a], refers to

LLM-generated text often replaces simple constructions that use copulas such as is or are with constructions such as serves as a or mark the. This pattern has been observed in GPT and Gemini models.[16] One study documented an over 10% decrease in the usage of the words is and are in academic writing in 2023, with no major changes in their frequency before that.[22] Similarly, LLMs prefer to use marketing-related verbs like features, offers, and the like to their neutral synonym has. (Note: Do not confuse this with has used in the past perfect form, as in has been featured.) Sometimes--especially in more recent AI output--these constructions are more elaborate, e.g., ventured into politics as a candidate versus was a candidate, or began his career as versus was.

A similar decline in "is"/"are" constructions has been observed on Wikipedia, especially when controlling for lead paragraphs (which usually follow a formulaic structure of "[article subject] is...]" and thus skew the data).[16] It is particularly visible in AI copyedits, which will often "improve" text in this way. The study above also demonstrated that when GPT-3.5 was prompted to "Revise the following sentence" in 10,000 abstracts, the words is and are appeared less often in the revised versions.[22]

In lead sentences, LLMs will sometimes avoid is by writing refers to as though the article were about the word or term instead of the subject directly.

### Curly quotation marks and apostrophes

> For non-AI-specific guidance about this, see Wikipedia:Manual of Style § Quotation characters, and Wikipedia:Manual of Style § Apostrophes.

> **Shortcut:** `WP:AICURLY`

ChatGPT and DeepSeek typically use curly quotation marks (“...” or ‘...’) instead of straight quotation marks ("..." or '...'). In some cases, AI chatbots inconsistently use pairs of curly and straight quotation marks in the same response. They also tend to use the curly apostrophe (’), the same character as the curly right single quotation mark, instead of the straight apostrophe ('), such as in contractions and possessive forms. They may also do this inconsistently.

Curly quotes alone do not prove LLM use. Directional quotation marks (curly or typographer) are often used in published works written and edited using the Chicago Manual of Style.[27] Microsoft Word has a "smart quotes" feature that converts straight quotes to curly quotes. So does the default system-wide configuration on macOS and iOS devices, except on some applications (or if turned off, as may be necessary for programming). Grammar correcting tools such as LanguageTool may also have such a feature. Curly quotation marks and apostrophes are common in professionally typeset works such as major newspapers. Citation tools like Citer may repeat those that appear in the title of a web page: for example,

McClelland, Mac (2017-09-27). "When ‘Not Guilty’ Is a Life Sentence". The New York Times. Retrieved 2025-08-03.

Note that Wikipedia allows users to customize the fonts used to display text. Some fonts display matched curly apostrophes as straight, in which case the distinction is invisible to the user. Additionally, Gemini and Claude models typically do not use curly quotes.

### Knowledge-cutoff disclaimers and speculation about gaps in sources

> **Shortcuts:** `WP:AICUTOFF` · `WP:AIDISCLAIMER`

> **Words to watch:** as of [date],[c] Up to my last training update, as of my last knowledge update, While specific details are limited/scarce..., not widely available/documented/disclosed, ...in the provided/available sources/search results..., based on available information ...

A knowledge-cutoff disclaimer is a statement used by an AI chatbot to indicate that the information provided may be incomplete, inaccurate, or outdated.

If an LLM has a fixed knowledge cutoff, such as older large language models like GPT-3.5 or GPT-4 (usually the model's last training update), it is unable to provide any information on events or developments past that time. Older LLMs would often remind the user about this by outputting a disclaimer that the information in its response is accurate only up to a certain date, and may explicitly mention the knowledge cutoff in doing so.

Newer chatbots with retrieval-augmented generation may also fail to find sources on a given topic, or to find information within the sources a user provides. In these cases, they may output a statement, similar to a knowledge-cutoff disclaimer, claiming that the information is not publicly available. They may also pair it with text about what that information "likely" may be and why it is significant. This information is entirely speculative (including the very claim that it's "not documented") and may be based on loosely related topics or completely fabricated. When that unknown information is about an individual's personal life, this disclaimer often claims that the person "maintains a low profile", "keeps personal details private", etc. This is also speculative.

## Markup

### Use of Markdown

> **Shortcuts:** `WP:MARKDOWN` · `WP:AIMARKDOWN`

A lot of AI chatbots are not proficient in wikitext, the markup language used to instruct Wikipedia's MediaWiki software how to format an article. As wikitext is a niche markup language, found mostly on wikis running on MediaWiki and other MediaWiki-based platforms like Miraheze, LLMs wikitext-formatted content is not prominent in their training data. While the corpora of chatbots did ingest millions of Wikipedia articles, these articles would not have been processed as text files containing wikitext syntax.

In chatbot apps, the output display is formatted with Markdown, a markup language conceptually similar to wikitext but much more widely applied. Meanwhile, the chatbots' preprompts typically instruct them to use markdown in their answers, such as when providing lists and writing with headings. That is, their system-level instructions often direct them to format outputs using Markdown, and the chatbot apps render its syntax as formatted text on a user's screen. For example, the system prompt for Claude Sonnet 3.5 (November 2024) includes:[28]

Claude uses Markdown formatting. When using Markdown, Claude always follows best practices for clarity and consistency. It always uses a single space after hash symbols for headers (e.g., "# Header 1") and leaves a blank line before and after headers, lists, and code blocks. For emphasis, Claude uses asterisks or underscores consistently (e.g., italic or bold). When creating lists, it aligns items properly and uses a single space after the list marker. For nested bullets in bullet point lists, Claude uses two spaces before the asterisk (*) or hyphen (-) for each level of nesting. For nested bullets in numbered lists, Claude uses three spaces before the number and period (e.g., "1.") for each level of nesting.

As the above indicates, Markdown syntax is completely different from wikitext. Markdown uses asterisks (*) or underscores (_) instead of single-quotes (') for bold and italic formatting, hash symbols (#) instead of equals signs (=) for section headings, parentheses (()) instead of square brackets ([]) around URLs, and three symbols (---, ***, or ___) instead of four hyphens (----) for thematic breaks.

When told to "generate an article", chatbots often default to using Markdown for the generated output. This formatting is preserved in clipboard text by the copy functions on some chatbot platforms. If instructed to generate content for Wikipedia, the chatbot might "realize" the need to generate Wikipedia-compatible code, and might include a message like Would you like me to ... turn this into actual Wikipedia markup format (`wikitext`)?[d] in its output. If the chatbot is told to proceed, the resulting syntax is often rudimentary, syntactically incorrect, or both. The chatbot might put its attempted-wikitext content in a Markdown-style fenced code block (its syntax for WP:PRE) surrounded by Markdown-based syntax and content, which may also be preserved by platform-specific copy-to-clipboard functions, leading to a telling footprint of both markup languages' syntax. This might include the appearance of three backticks in the text, such as: ```wikitext.[e][f]

The presence of faulty wikitext syntax mixed with Markdown syntax is a strong indicator that content is LLM-generated, especially if in the form of a fenced Markdown code block. However, Markdown alone is not such a strong indicator. Software developers, researchers, technical writers, and experienced internet users frequently use Markdown in tools like Obsidian and GitHub, and on platforms like Reddit, Discord, and Slack. Some writing tools and apps, such as iOS Notes, Google Docs, and Windows Notepad, support Markdown editing or exporting. The increasing ubiquity of Markdown may also lead new editors to expect or assume Wikipedia to support Markdown by default.

### Broken external links

If a new article or draft has multiple citations with external links, and several of them are broken (e.g., non-existent websites or 404 errors), this is a strong sign of an AI-generated page, particularly if the dead links are not found in website archiving sites like the Internet Archive. Most links become broken over time, but these factors make it unlikely that the link was ever real.

Watch out for: Links that don't work for you, but do work for other people (e.g., journal articles accessed through a university library); links that were mangled by bots and scripts (e.g., to add incorrect identifiers or to remove seemingly unnecessary parts of the URL); links that are missing the start or end (a sign of a human copy/pasting the URL).

### Invalid DOI and ISBNs

> For non-AI-specific guidance about DOI and ISBNs, see Wikipedia:DOI and Wikipedia:ISBN.

A checksum can be used to verify ISBNs. An invalid checksum is a very likely sign that an ISBN is incorrect, and citation templates display a warning if so. Similarly, DOIs are more resistant to link rot than regular hyperlinks. Unresolvable DOIs and invalid ISBNs can be indicators of hallucinated references.

### DOIs that lead to unrelated articles

A LLM may generate references to non-existent scholarly articles with DOIs that appear valid but are, in reality, assigned to unrelated articles. Example passage generated by ChatGPT:

Ohm’s Law applies to many materials and components that are "ohmic," meaning their resistance remains constant regardless of the applied voltage or current. However, it does not hold for non-linear devices like diodes or transistors [1][2].

1. M. E. Van Valkenburg, “The validity and limitations of Ohm’s law in non-linear circuits,” Proceedings of the IEEE, vol. 62, no. 6, pp. 769–770, Jun. 1974. doi:10.1109/PROC.1974.9547

2. C. L. Fortescue, “Ohm’s Law in alternating current circuits,” Proceedings of the IEEE, vol. 55, no. 11, pp. 1934–1936, Nov. 1967. doi:10.1109/PROC.1967.6033

Both Proceedings of the IEEE citations are completely made up. The DOIs lead to different citations and have other problems as well. For instance, C. L. Fortescue was dead for 30+ years at the purported time of writing, and Vol 55, Issue 11 does not list any articles that match anything remotely close to the information given in reference 2.

> **Note:** From 2018 to 2023, a UX issue in VisualEditor led many editors to accidentally insert references to PubMed articles with low ID numbers (PMIDs), resulting in obviously irrelevant citations like an article about rat livers (PMID 9) being cited in List of Disney television films in 2020. These may resemble AI hallucinations (and should be fixed regardless), but they generally are not.

Some LLM-generated book citations include page numbers, and the book exists, but the cited pages do not verify the text. Signs to look out for: the book is on a somewhat general topic or frequently referenced in its field, and the citation does not include a URL (not mandatory for book citations, but editors creating legitimate book citations often include a link to an online version of the text). ## Miscellaneous

### Pronounced shift in writing style

A sudden shift in an editor's writing style, such as unexpectedly flawless grammar compared to their other communication (e.g., talk page comments versus text added), may indicate the use of AI. This is especially likely if that other writing predates November 2022. The reverse also applies: If a user has edits that predate LLM chatbots, and their writing style has remained consistent between those older edits and their current ones (e.g., frequent use of boldface, list formatting, etc.), that suggests the newer edits are less likely to be AI.

A mismatch of user location, national ties of the topic to a variety of English, and the variety of English used may indicate the use of AI tools. A human writer from India writing about an Indian university would probably not use American English; however, several LLMs use American English by default unless prompted otherwise.[31]

Note that non-native English speakers also tend to mix up English varieties, and many writers use more formal prose in certain venues as a form of code switching (although that doesn't rule out writers doing that code switching with AI). These signs should raise suspicion only if there is a dramatic, not easily explainable shift in an editor's writing style.

More subtly, if an editor has used AI for several years, the writing style of their edits will often change in parallel with contemporaneous AI tools: their 2023 edits will resemble 2023 LLM output, their 2025 edits will resemble 2025 LLM output, and so on.

### Overwhelmingly exhaustive edit summaries

> **Shortcuts:** `WP:AISUMMARY` · `WP:AISUMM`

AI-generated edit summaries are often written as formal, first-person paragraphs, without abbreviations, and conspicuously echo the exact text of Wikipedia's policies or any maintenance tags on the article—for example, itemizing their adherence to "WP:NPOV" or "encyclopedic tone." ### Permissions gaming

Permissions gaming is a form of disruptive editing where someone makes many benign-seeming but unconstructive edits, often on disparate topics or in quick succession, until their edit count is high enough to raise their user access level, allowing them to pursue their real goal of adding spam, vandalism, or contentious content.

Because AI chatbots are good at generating a lot of benign-looking content very quickly, people who game permissions after 2023 often do so with throwaway AI rewrites or additions to dozens of unrelated articles. If no one goes back and cleans up their edits, the result is a large swath of undetected and unreviewed AI content.

> **Note:** This sign should only be used in one direction. Someone rapidly adding a lot of AI-generated text is not necessarily permissions gaming and should not be accused of it barring other evidence. However, if someone is found or reasonably suspected to be permissions gaming, and has done so by rapidly adding or changing a lot of text, those edits may be AI.

## Differences between LLMs

Each model and version of AI chatbots have a distinctive way of writing (idiolect),[32][10], and what is typical for GPT-5 is not necessarily characteristic of GPT-4 or Gemini.

For example, text from ChatGPT (circa GPT-4o-2024-08-06) and Grok (Grok-Beta, as of late 2024/early 2025) exhibit characteristics that Gemini (1-5 Pro) and Claude (3.5-Sonnet) do not:

focusing on broader context is more characteristic of ChatGPT and Grok than Gemini and Claude.[10]

Gemini and Claude responses tend to be more concise than responses from ChatGPT and Grok.[i][10]

Though it's impossible to know for sure and there are many confounding variables, ChatGPT is likely the most widely used chatbot for Wikipedia edits.[33]

## Signs of human writing

### Age of text relative to ChatGPT launch

ChatGPT was launched to the public on November 30, 2022. Although OpenAI had similarly powerful LLMs before then, they were paid services and not easily accessible or known to lay people. Thus, if an edit was made before November 30, 2022, AI use can be safely ruled out for the corresponding text. While some older writing displays some of the AI signs given in this list, and may even convincingly appear to have been AI-generated, the vastness of Wikipedia allows for these coincidences.

### Ability to explain one's own editorial choices

Editors should be able to explain why they made an edit or mistake. For example, if an editor inserts a URL that appears fabricated, you can ask how the mix-up occurred instead of jumping to conclusions. If they can supply the correct link and explain it as a human error (perhaps a typo), or share the relevant passage from the real source, that points to an ordinary human error.

### Syntax

LLMs writing or editing Wikipedia articles will attempt, by default, to produce text in what it considers to be "formal, neutral, encyclopedic tone." This manifests as AI-generated text avoiding certain syntactic constructions that are both common in human writing and often perfectly acceptable per the Manual of Style--and in some cases, even preferred by it.

Specifically, the following have been empirically observed, over 25 years of Wikipedia writing, to be more common in Wikipedia articles written by humans than in AI-generated text:

Simple is/has phrases,[22] such as there is a, it has a.

Words with complex, stiff or euphemistic synonyms, such as wrote (versus authored), moved (versus relocated), used (versus utilized), tried (versus attempted), died (versus passed away).

### Superlative or definitive statements, such as one of the best, is the only, was the first

Hedging qualifiers and intensifiers,[34] such as very, perhaps, tends to.

Isolated wordy constructions such as as a result of, in order to, all of the, a part of, or the fact that.

## Ineffective indicators

False accusations of AI use can drive away new editors and foster an atmosphere of suspicion. Before claiming AI was used, consider whether the Dunning–Kruger effect or confirmation bias may be clouding your judgement. Detecting LLM texts on the basis of style alone is not as easy as it seems, see WP:AIDETECTIVE in this page. Here are several somewhat commonly used indicators that are ineffective in LLM detection—and may even indicate the opposite.

Perfect grammar: While modern LLMs are known for high grammatical proficiency, many editors are also skilled writers or come from professional writing backgrounds. (See also § Sudden shift in English variety use.)

Combination of casual and formal registers, or language that sounds both "clinical" and "emotional": This may indicate the casual writing of a person in a technical field, such as computer science. It may also indicate youth, a preference for mixed registers, playfulness, or neurodivergence. In the case of a wiki, it may simply be the result of multiple editors adding to a page.

"Bland" or "robotic" prose: LLM output has specific traits, as detailed above, and it skews positive and verbose by default. While these tendencies are formulaic, they may not scan as "robotic" to those unfamiliar with AI writing.[35]

"Fancy", "academic", or "formal" prose: While LLMs disproportionately favor certain words and phrases, many of which are longer and have more difficult readability scores than some of their synonyms, these are specific words. The correlation does not extend to all formal, academic, or "fancy"-sounding prose.[1]

Letter-like writing (in isolation): Although many talk page messages written with salutations, valedictions, subject lines, and other formalities after 2023 tend to appear AI-generated, letters and emails have conventionally been written in such ways long before modern LLMs existed. Human editors (particularly newer editors) may format their talk page comments similarly for various reasons, such as being more accustomed to formal communication, posting as part of a school assignment that requires this tone, or simply mistaking the talk page for email. Other tells, such as vertical lists[j], placeholders, or abrupt cutoffs, are stronger.

Transition words (in isolation): Older AI text tended to formulaically overuse certain transitions like Additionally, Consequently, and Notably, often to begin sentences. However, only a few transition words and phrases are known to be overused by AI in this way. This pattern also has precedence in essay-like writing by humans and is accepted by many style guides, so this is not a strong tell.

Unsourced content: More than 570,000 articles are tagged as needing citations, and most of them predate LLMs. Meanwhile, since modern LLM chatbots can search the web and view sources a user provides to it, citations are fairly common now in AI-generated text. This does not mean they are accurate citations, but they are there.

Bizarre wikitext: While LLMs may hallucinate templates or generate wikitext code with invalid syntax for reasons explained in § Use of Markdown, they are not likely to generate content with certain random-seeming, "inexplicable" errors and artifacts (excluding the ones listed here in § Markup). Bizarrely placed HTML tags like <span> are more indicative of poorly programmed browser extensions or a known bug with Wikipedia's content translation tool (T113137). Misplaced syntax like ''Catch-22 i''s a satirical novel. (rendered as "Catch-22 is a satirical novel.") are more indicative of mistakes in VisualEditor, where such errors are harder to notice than in source editing.

Correct wikitext: Especially if the person is using the visual editor or has found the Preview button, getting the formatting correct, even for complex templates, is normal.

### Prompt refusal

> **Words to watch:** as an AI language model, as a large language model, I cannot offer medical advice, but I can..., I'm sorry ...

In the past, AI chatbots occasionally declined to answer prompts as written, usually with apologies and reminders that they are AI language models. Attempting to be helpful, chatbots often gave suggestions or answers to alternative, similar requests. Outright refusals have become increasingly rare.

#### Examples

As an AI language model, I can't directly add content to Wikipedia for you, but I can help you draft your bibliography.

> *— From this 2024 revision to Parmiter's Almshouse & Pension Charity*

### Abrupt cut offs

AI tools used to abruptly stop generating content if an excessive number of tokens had been used for a single response, and further responses required the user to select "continue generating", at least in the case of ChatGPT.

This method is not foolproof, as a malformed copy/paste from one's local computer can also cause this. It may also indicate a copyright violation rather than the use of an LLM.

### Outdated access-date parameters

In some AI-assisted text, citations may include an access-date by default, but the date can look unexpectedly old relative to when the edit was made (for example, an article created in December 2025 containing multiple citations with |access-date=12 December 2024). However, newer chatbots seldom produce this error, and older access-date values can occur legitimately (copied citations, offline work, batch moves/merges).

# Research supplement: natural human prose, historical baselines, and AI-associated writing patterns

**Added:** 17 September 2026  
**Status:** Research supplement. The material above is preserved unchanged. This section is not Wikipedia policy and does not replace the caveats in the original document.

## Purpose and limits

The original document is primarily a field guide for recognizing possible signs of AI-generated writing on Wikipedia. It repeatedly warns that the signs are descriptive rather than prescriptive and that no single stylistic feature proves AI authorship. That distinction should remain central when this material is used outside Wikipedia.

This supplement has a different purpose: it collects practical lessons from pre-LLM English prose, long-standing style guidance, and empirical research on modern LLM writing. The goal is better writing that sounds situated, specific, and authored rather than generic, over-smoothed, or templated.

There is no foolproof recipe for making a text "undetectable." Human judgments and automated detectors both make errors. A 2025 study of 63 lecturers found recognition rates of about 57% for AI text and 64% for human text in short academic passages. Earlier Stanford research also found substantial false positives for non-native English writers. Treat detector output as weak evidence unless it is supported by provenance, drafting history, factual verification, or other independent evidence.[S9][S10]

A useful principle is therefore:

> **Do not write for a detector. Write for the reader, the facts, the genre, and the actual authorial purpose.**

Attempts to manipulate a detector by adding mistakes, random slang, invisible characters, strange punctuation, fake anecdotes, or arbitrary sentence-length variation can reduce quality without establishing genuine authorship.

## 1. Historical baseline: human English was never one style

A reliable pre-AI baseline must be broader than one author or one century. Human prose has always included formal and informal registers, long and short sentences, heavy and light punctuation, repetition, parallelism, fragments, rhetorical questions, digressions, and strongly individual habits.

Old English, roughly the English of the early medieval period, is not a useful direct template for contemporary prose because its grammar, vocabulary, spelling, and syntax differ substantially from modern English. For present-day writing, a more useful historical comparison begins with Early Modern and Modern English and then samples several genres.

### 1.1 Francis Bacon: compressed, aphoristic prose

Bacon's *Essays*, first published in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries and expanded in 1625, show that unmistakably human prose can be highly compressed, balanced, formal, and full of parallel structures. Human writing is not defined by casualness or grammatical imperfection.

What matters for a modern writer is the relationship between form and thought. Bacon's compression works because a sentence carries a definite claim. The sentence is not merely decorated to sound important.

**Lesson:** Parallelism is not an AI tell by itself. Repeated symmetry becomes suspicious only when it appears automatically, regardless of subject, and adds little meaning.

### 1.2 Addison and Steele: conversational public prose

*The Spectator* essays of 1711 and 1712 provide a different baseline. They frequently move between observation, anecdote, opinion, direct reader address, social detail, and humor. The prose is orderly, but it does not sound like a uniform information product.[S3]

**Lesson:** Human prose often changes pace because the writer is responding to the material. A factual passage may be followed by a short judgment, an anecdote, or a question. The shift has a reason.

### 1.3 Nineteenth-century prose: personality survives correctness

Writers such as Mark Twain and Robert Louis Stevenson show two useful properties of human style. Twain's criticism is concrete, opinionated, comic, and example-driven. Stevenson treats prose style as a craft involving word choice, sentence shape, rhythm, and sound rather than a checklist of ornamental vocabulary.[S4][S5]

The important point is not to imitate either writer. It is to notice that their prose contains decisions. A phrase exists because that writer wanted that exact effect.

**Lesson:** Distinctive prose comes from recurring preferences that serve meaning, not from randomly injecting quirks.

### 1.4 Strunk: specificity and economy are older than AI

William Strunk's early twentieth-century *The Elements of Style* recommends active voice, definite and concrete language, and the removal of unnecessary words. It also warns against a succession of similarly built loose sentences.[S2]

Strunk's rule about concision is often oversimplified. He does not say that every sentence should be short. He explicitly explains that concision does not require cutting all detail or reducing every sentence to a minimal form. The goal is for each word to do work.

**Lesson:** A natural paragraph may contain a long sentence when the idea needs one. Artificially forcing every sentence into the same short range creates its own mechanical rhythm.

### 1.5 Orwell: prefabricated language is the deeper problem

George Orwell's 1946 essay *Politics and the English Language* predates modern generative AI by decades but describes a problem that maps closely onto many current AI-writing complaints: stale imagery, abstract wording, inflated diction, ready-made phrases, and loss of precision. He argues for choosing words because of their meaning rather than accepting prefabricated phrases.[S1]

This is more useful than a blacklist. A phrase is weak not because a detector might associate it with AI, but because the writer has stopped making a fresh decision.

**Lesson:** The strongest "humanizing" edit is often to replace a generic phrase with the exact fact, action, object, person, date, number, or consequence that the phrase was hiding.

## 2. The strongest general principle: specificity beats generic significance

Many AI-associated passages have a peculiar combination of vagueness and certainty. They say that something is important, pivotal, transformative, vibrant, evolving, or representative without giving enough concrete evidence for that judgment.

Compare the underlying logic:

**Generic significance**
> The project represents a significant step in the evolving landscape of urban mobility.

**Specific statement**
> The project added 42 electric buses to routes 7, 11, and 18 and cut the scheduled headway on route 11 from 20 minutes to 12.

The second version does not need to announce significance. The reader can see what changed.

This principle appears in both historical style advice and the uploaded document. It also explains why merely replacing words such as "pivotal" or "landscape" with synonyms does not solve the problem. "Important," "major," and "field" can be equally empty if the sentence still lacks information.

### Practical test

For any evaluative sentence, ask:

1. What happened?
2. Who did it?
3. When?
4. Where?
5. By how much?
6. According to whom?
7. Compared with what?
8. What evidence would make the judgment unnecessary?

If the factual answer can replace the abstract judgment, prefer the fact.

## 3. Modern empirical research: patterns are statistical, not verdicts

### 3.1 Excess vocabulary exists, but single words do not identify authorship

A large study of more than 15 million PubMed abstracts from 2010 through 2024 found a sharp post-LLM increase in many style words. In 2024, examples with high excess-frequency ratios included "delves," "underscores," and "showcasing." More common words such as "potential," "findings," and "crucial" also rose beyond the researchers' pre-LLM trend estimates.[S7]

The same study is explicit about an important limitation: its analysis works at corpus level and cannot identify whether an individual abstract was processed by an LLM.[S7]

That leads to a better rule:

> **Watch clusters, density, and context. Do not treat an ordinary English word as forbidden.**

A human writer may naturally use "crucial." An LLM-like passage is more likely to stack several fashionable abstractions, promotional verbs, and significance claims in a short space.

### 3.2 Model style is multidimensional

Research comparing human and LLM writing finds differences across many grammatical and rhetorical features rather than one magic token.[S8] A 2026 large-scale preprint using 11 LLMs and eight genres reports that genre can affect style more strongly than source, while some differences between human and machine text remain across prompting conditions.[S11]

This matters because "human writing" is not a single target style. A Reddit post, legal memo, product page, academic abstract, personal email, and technical incident report should not be made to sound alike.

**Rule:** Match the genre before trying to match an imagined universal human voice.

### 3.3 Human variability should come from purpose, not randomization

Some people try to make prose look human by manipulating "burstiness" or by alternating short and long sentences mechanically. This confuses a statistical observation with a writing method.

Real variation is usually functional:

- a short sentence can state a conclusion;
- a longer sentence can hold conditions, qualifications, or a sequence;
- a fragment can work in dialogue or informal notes;
- a technical definition may need stable terminology;
- a narrative paragraph may change pace with events.

Random variation is another template.

## 4. Em dashes: use, overuse, and the AI association

### 4.1 An em dash is legitimate English punctuation

The em dash (—) existed long before modern LLMs. Chicago describes it as a flexible mark that can set off an additional thought, mark an interruption, or substitute for other punctuation in some contexts. A hyphen (-), en dash (–), and em dash (—) have different functions.[S12]

Therefore:

**Do not replace an em dash with a hyphen simply to look less like AI.**  
A hyphen is not a short em dash. It normally joins elements inside a compound or helps prevent ambiguity.

### 4.2 There is evidence of an LLM-era frequency shift in one domain

A preregistered 2026 preprint analyzed 146,239 U.S. congressional press releases from 480 House and Senate offices covering 2021 through 2025. The density of unspaced prose-form em dashes stayed around 0.10 to 0.12 per 1,000 characters through 2024, then rose to 0.217 in 2025. The share of releases containing that form rose from about 13% to 24.8%.[S13]

The author also states that this is a **population-level marker, not a per-release authorship detector**, and that the design does not establish a causal claim.[S13]

That is the correct level of confidence.

### 4.3 Better punctuation practice

Use an em dash when the interruption or parenthetical force is genuinely stronger than a comma and less detached than a parenthesis.

Consider alternatives before using it:

- **comma** for a light interruption;
- **colon** when the second part explains, specifies, or delivers the first;
- **semicolon** between closely related independent clauses;
- **parentheses** for information that is genuinely secondary;
- **period** when the second thought deserves its own sentence.

If a paragraph contains several em dashes doing the same rhetorical job, revise some of them. This is an editorial heuristic, not a numeric detection rule.

### 4.4 Spacing is a style choice, not authorship evidence

Different house styles use different spacing around dashes. Chicago normally uses unspaced em dashes. Other editorial traditions use spaced dashes. A document should follow its house style consistently.

Do not change punctuation spacing solely to manipulate an AI detector.

## 5. Hyphens: necessary grammar, unnecessary clutter

A hyphen usually connects words that function together, resolves ambiguity, or follows a particular compound-word convention. Chicago advises sparing hyphenation but also notes that a hyphen can improve legibility when a compound would otherwise be misread.[S12][S14]

### Legitimate uses

Examples include:

- a well-known result;
- a five-year plan;
- a risk-based approach;
- a user-facing setting;
- re-create when necessary to distinguish it from recreate, depending on style.

### Where prose starts to look manufactured

The problem is not the punctuation mark. It is stacked branding language such as:

> a future-ready, insight-driven, customer-centric, high-impact platform

This structure often combines several vague compound modifiers whose main function is promotional.

A better version names the function:

> The platform imports sales data, flags duplicate accounts, and sends the assigned representative a daily exception list.

### Practical rule

Use a hyphen because the compound needs one, not because compound adjectives sound polished.

## 6. Curly quotes, apostrophes, and typography

Curly quotation marks and apostrophes are not useful proof of AI authorship. Professional typesetting, word processors, operating systems, and style guides have used them for decades.

Treat typography as house style:

- use straight quotes in code and formats that require them;
- use typographic quotes in edited prose if the publication uses them;
- keep the convention consistent.

The same principle applies to en dashes, ellipses, serial commas, and date formatting.

## 7. Sentence rhythm: avoid the metronome

LLM prose often feels smooth because every sentence arrives fully balanced and approximately the same rhetorical size. Human prose can also be smooth, but strong human writing commonly lets sentence form follow the thought.

### Warning signs of mechanical rhythm

- five consecutive sentences of similar length;
- every sentence containing one comma and a trailing participial phrase;
- every paragraph beginning with a topic sentence and ending with a significance sentence;
- repeated "X is..., This..., Additionally..., Moreover..., Overall..." progression;
- repeated semicolon patterns;
- a sequence of three-part lists in every section;
- every paragraph having nearly the same number of sentences.

### Better practice

Do not vary syntax for its own sake. Instead, change the grammatical shape when the intellectual job changes.

Use:

- a direct declarative sentence for a fact;
- a conditional sentence for a condition;
- a question only when a real question is being raised;
- a subordinate clause when one fact depends on another;
- a list when readers actually need to scan separate items;
- a short sentence when compression adds force.

The sentence should reveal the structure of the thought.

## 8. Repetition is often more human and clearer than "elegant variation"

LLMs are often pushed to avoid repetition, which can produce needless synonym swapping:

> the company ... the firm ... the organization ... the enterprise ...

In factual writing, repeated terminology may be clearer. The UK Office for National Statistics explicitly advises using consistent terms for the same idea rather than swapping near-synonyms that may carry different meanings.[S15]

This is especially important in:

- technical documentation;
- contracts;
- academic methods;
- product requirements;
- incident reports;
- data models;
- instructions.

**Rule:** Repeat the correct noun when precision benefits from repetition. Vary words when the meaning or emphasis genuinely changes.

## 9. Ordinary verbs are a strength

The uploaded document already notes the tendency to replace simple "is," "are," and "has" constructions with inflated alternatives such as "serves as," "stands as," "features," or "boasts."

The older style tradition reaches the same conclusion from a different direction. Orwell criticizes verbal padding and inflated substitutes for simple verbs. Strunk favors direct constructions.

Prefer the ordinary verb when it says exactly what happened:

- "wrote" rather than "authored" when no distinction is needed;
- "used" rather than "utilized";
- "helped" rather than "facilitated" when "helped" is accurate;
- "is" rather than "serves as" when identity is all that is being stated;
- "has" rather than "boasts" or "features" when possession is the meaning;
- "started" rather than "commenced" unless the register genuinely calls for it.

Do not turn this into another blacklist. "Facilitate" has legitimate meanings. The problem is choosing a grander verb without gaining precision.

## 10. Cut significance tails

A common AI-like sentence has a factual first half and an unnecessary interpretive tail:

> The archive opened in 1987, **highlighting the institution's enduring commitment to preserving cultural memory.**

The tail often does one of four things:

1. praises the subject;
2. declares broad significance;
3. invents a causal implication;
4. restates the fact abstractly.

Delete the tail unless a source actually supports the interpretation and the interpretation matters.

A useful editing operation is:

**fact + generic significance tail -> fact**

This single change often improves neutrality, density, and credibility.

## 11. Be suspicious of automatic participial endings

The uploaded document correctly identifies a recurring construction in which a sentence ends with an "-ing" phrase:

> ..., highlighting ...
> ..., underscoring ...
> ..., reflecting ...
> ..., reinforcing ...
> ..., demonstrating ...

These endings are grammatically valid. They become weak when the participial phrase is not a necessary consequence of the main clause.

### Test

Replace the final phrase with a question:

> Does this fact actually demonstrate that claim?

If the answer requires another argument, source, or assumption, the participial tail is doing hidden analytical work.

Either prove the relationship or remove it.

## 12. Avoid fake comprehensiveness

AI systems are rewarded for being helpful and complete. This can produce unnecessary taxonomies, three-part frameworks, exhaustive-looking lists, and headings for ideas that need only a sentence.

Human writers also use lists and frameworks. The difference is necessity.

Before making a list, ask:

- Are these items genuinely parallel?
- Does the reader need to compare them?
- Would prose be easier?
- Did I choose three items because there are exactly three, or because three feels rhetorically complete?
- Is the final item included only to make the set look comprehensive?

Do not add a third point merely to satisfy the rule of three.

## 13. Avoid template transitions

Transitions should explain a relationship, not announce that another sentence is coming.

Potentially mechanical openings include:

- Additionally,
- Moreover,
- Furthermore,
- Notably,
- Importantly,
- In today's rapidly evolving...
- In the realm of...
- In an increasingly...
- It is worth noting that...
- It is important to note that...
- Overall,
- In conclusion,

None is forbidden. The problem is frequency and predictability.

Often the transition can disappear:

> Additionally, the API accepts CSV files.

becomes:

> The API also accepts CSV files.

Or simply:

> The API accepts CSV files.

When a relationship matters, name the relationship:

> **Because** the API validates the schema before upload, malformed rows never reach the warehouse.

The conjunction now carries logic rather than decoration.

## 14. Avoid false balance and automatic contrast

LLMs often generate polished contrast templates:

- not only X but also Y;
- not just X, but Y;
- while X, Y;
- despite X, Y;
- rather than X, Y;
- although X, it is important to...

These are normal English constructions. The problem is using them before the text has established a real contrast.

### Test

Write the two propositions separately. If neither depends on the other, the contrast may be decorative.

Instead of:

> The redesign is not just visually cleaner but also significantly improves usability.

Write what changed:

> The redesign reduced the checkout form from nine required fields to five. In a usability test, 17 of 20 participants completed the task without assistance, compared with 11 of 20 in the earlier version.

## 15. Keep uncertainty local and specific

Human writing often contains uncertainty. The uploaded document rightly warns against generic knowledge disclaimers such as "based on available information" followed by speculation.

Good uncertainty states exactly what is unknown and why:

> The minutes record the vote but do not identify how each member voted.

Weak uncertainty expands into a vague performance of caution:

> While specific information remains limited, available sources suggest that the decision may have reflected a broader range of strategic considerations.

The first sentence tells the reader the evidentiary boundary. The second fills the boundary with speculation.

## 16. Attribution should name an actual source or actor

Avoid:

- experts say;
- observers note;
- critics argue;
- industry reports suggest;
- it is widely believed;
- many sources indicate.

Use:

> In its 2025 annual report, the company attributed the delay to a failed supplier qualification test.

If several sources genuinely agree, cite or identify enough of them to justify the collective description.

Do not upgrade one source into "researchers," "critics," or "the literature."

## 17. Do not add fake intimacy or fake personal experience

A common attempt to sound human is to add invented experiences:

> I remember struggling with this exact problem...

Do not fabricate experience, emotion, memory, sensory detail, or personal history.

If personal voice is appropriate, use only real information supplied by the actual author. Otherwise, clear impersonal prose is better than counterfeit personality.

## 18. Do not add deliberate mistakes

Typos, missing articles, random lowercase letters, broken grammar, and inconsistent punctuation do not establish human authorship.

They can:

- damage credibility;
- create accessibility problems;
- confuse non-native readers;
- make technical material unsafe;
- trigger a different kind of automated quality flag.

Natural human writing includes errors because humans make errors, not because good writers schedule them.

## 19. Do not optimize "perplexity" or "burstiness" as writing goals

Some detector advice tells writers to increase perplexity, burstiness, lexical diversity, or unpredictability.

These are measurement concepts, not sound editorial objectives. Deliberately maximizing unpredictability can produce obscure vocabulary and unstable syntax. Deliberately maximizing sentence-length variance can produce a visibly engineered pattern.

Write the sentence that best expresses the idea. Statistical variation should be an outcome of real decisions.

## 20. Preserve the author's stable idiolect

One of the strongest signs of genuine authorship is continuity across time and context.

An individual's style may include recurring preferences such as:

- British or American spelling;
- contractions or few contractions;
- short introductions;
- frequent parenthetical qualifications;
- favored technical terms;
- a particular degree of formality;
- preferred heading style;
- characteristic sentence lengths;
- recurring but legitimate punctuation habits.

Do not erase these merely because a generic style guide prefers something else.

When editing another person's draft, compare the revision with that person's earlier writing. A sudden unexplained shift toward polished generic prose can be more suspicious than any single word.

## 21. Genre first: a human email should not sound like a human research paper

A major error in "humanizing" advice is using the same rules for every genre.

### Email

Human professional email often uses:

- a direct reason for writing;
- context known to both parties;
- incomplete repetition of already-known facts;
- practical requests;
- ordinary verbs;
- contractions where relationship and culture permit them.

It does not need an essay introduction and conclusion.

### Technical documentation

Good technical writing often repeats exact terms and follows strict structure. That consistency is not an AI tell. Precision is more important than stylistic novelty.

### Academic writing

Academic prose legitimately uses cautious language, nominalizations, passive voice, technical vocabulary, and citations. The problem is not formality. The problem is unsupported generalization, formulaic significance claims, or citations that do not support the sentence.

### Marketing

Marketing legitimately uses benefits and persuasion. It should still prefer evidence over stacks of generic positive adjectives.

### Wikipedia and encyclopedic prose

Neutrality, verifiability, source fidelity, and appropriate wikitext matter more than "sounding human." Many signs in the original document are Wikipedia-specific and should not be exported blindly to email, fiction, or product documentation.

### Personal or conversational writing

Fragments, contractions, direct questions, idioms, and uneven paragraph lengths may be natural here. They should not be artificially imported into a legal memo or research paper.

## 22. A practical drafting process that produces less generic prose

### Pass 1: write the factual spine

Before polishing, record:

- the main claim;
- the evidence;
- names;
- dates;
- numbers;
- actions;
- uncertainties;
- source boundaries.

Do not begin with ornamental prose.

### Pass 2: decide the paragraph's job

Each paragraph should have a reason to exist. Common jobs include:

- state a fact;
- explain a mechanism;
- compare two cases;
- give evidence;
- describe a sequence;
- qualify a claim;
- show a consequence.

If a paragraph is doing five jobs, divide it. If two paragraphs do the same job, combine them.

### Pass 3: choose verbs

Underline the main verb in each sentence.

Ask:

- Is it specific?
- Is it carrying the action?
- Did I turn the action into a noun and hide it behind "is," "provides," "enables," "facilitates," or "serves as"?
- Did I choose a grand verb only because it sounds professional?

### Pass 4: remove generic significance

Search for:

- important;
- significant;
- crucial;
- pivotal;
- key;
- transformative;
- groundbreaking;
- vibrant;
- robust;
- evolving;
- landscape;
- testament;
- underscores;
- highlights;
- showcases.

Do not delete them automatically. For each occurrence, demand evidence or a reason.

### Pass 5: inspect sentence endings

Search for final phrases beginning with:

- highlighting;
- underscoring;
- demonstrating;
- reflecting;
- showcasing;
- reinforcing;
- ensuring;
- contributing.

Check whether the logical connection is proved.

### Pass 6: inspect symmetry

Look for:

- repeated three-item lists;
- repeated "not X but Y";
- repeated "while X, Y";
- repeated sentence lengths;
- repeated paragraph templates;
- repeated transitions.

Break the pattern only when the content does not justify it.

### Pass 7: check terminology

If two terms mean the same thing, choose the technically correct term and repeat it. Do not rotate synonyms merely to avoid repetition.

### Pass 8: read aloud

Reading aloud exposes:

- clauses that are too crowded;
- rhythms that repeat;
- fake transitions;
- places where a simpler word is more natural;
- sentences that sound finished before they have said anything.

### Pass 9: verify sources

For every factual attribution:

- open the source;
- find the exact supporting passage;
- check date and context;
- confirm the source says what the prose claims;
- check that a collective claim is supported by multiple sources if it is described as collective.

### Pass 10: remove the performance of helpfulness

Delete chatbot residue that addresses an invisible user or describes the response rather than giving the content:

- "Here is a detailed breakdown";
- "Let's dive in";
- "I hope this helps";
- "Would you like me to";
- "Below is a comprehensive overview";
- "It is important to remember";
- "In this section, we will explore".

Use such language only when the real communication situation calls for it.

## 23. Editing diagnostics: questions that reveal generic prose

Use these questions on a finished paragraph.

1. Could this sentence be pasted into an article about a different subject with only the nouns changed?
2. Does the sentence announce importance instead of showing evidence?
3. Is the final clause an unsupported interpretation?
4. Did I use three items because there are exactly three?
5. Did I rename the same object only to avoid repeating a noun?
6. Is a transition word doing work that the logic should do?
7. Does every paragraph have the same shape?
8. Are several sentences close to the same length?
9. Did I choose a longer word where a common word is equally precise?
10. Is the subject of the sentence the actual actor?
11. Are passive constructions hiding responsibility?
12. Does "experts," "critics," or "researchers" refer to named people or sources?
13. Could a number, date, place, or concrete action replace an abstract adjective?
14. Does "this" have an obvious referent?
15. Am I explaining a fact twice, first concretely and then abstractly?
16. Did I write a conclusion merely because long responses are expected to have one?
17. Is a heading necessary, or would one sentence be clearer?
18. Does the punctuation reflect meaning, or am I using it for drama?
19. Does the text sound like this author in this genre?
20. Can every strong factual claim be traced to evidence?

## 24. False tells: features that should not be treated as proof

| Feature | What it actually means |
| --- | --- |
| One em dash | Normal punctuation. Weak evidence by itself. |
| No em dashes | Proves nothing. Many humans and many models avoid them. |
| Hyphens | Normal compound-word punctuation. Judge necessity, not presence. |
| Curly quotes | Often produced by Word, macOS, iOS, publishing systems, and house style. |
| Perfect grammar | Skilled human writers also write grammatical prose. |
| A long sentence | Human prose has always contained long sentences. |
| Short sentences | Also normal human prose. A whole document of uniform short sentences can become mechanical. |
| Formal vocabulary | Appropriate in many disciplines. Specific overused words may be statistical markers, but formality itself is not. |
| Passive voice | Legitimate when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or intentionally backgrounded. |
| First person | Normal in many genres and prohibited or discouraged in others. |
| Contractions | Genre-dependent. Neither presence nor absence establishes authorship. |
| Bullet lists | Useful for scan-oriented information. Mechanical list formatting can still look templated. |
| "Additionally" once | Ordinary English. Density and repeated placement matter more. |
| Repetition | Often improves technical clarity. |
| A conclusion | Necessary in some genres and redundant in others. |
| Markdown | Normal for developers, researchers, GitHub, Slack, Reddit, and many note systems. |
| American or British English | Usually reflects audience, house style, education, or locale. A sudden unexplained switch is more informative than either variety itself. |

## 25. Source-grounded human writing versus "human-like" decoration

A useful distinction:

**Source-grounded human writing**
- makes claims the evidence supports;
- contains details selected for a reason;
- preserves uncertainty;
- uses genre-appropriate structure;
- has identifiable editorial choices;
- can usually be explained by its author.

**Human-like decoration**
- random typos;
- arbitrary slang;
- fake hesitations;
- fake anecdotes;
- random fragments;
- deliberately inconsistent punctuation;
- forced sentence-length variation;
- replacing every suspected AI word with a synonym;
- adding personal pronouns with no real personal perspective.

The first improves authorship. The second performs authorship.

## 26. A stronger replacement for "AI word blacklists"

Instead of maintaining a list of forbidden words, classify suspect phrases by function.

### A. Significance inflation

Examples:
- pivotal;
- crucial;
- testament;
- enduring legacy;
- broader landscape.

**Edit:** replace with the concrete consequence or delete.

### B. Promotional gloss

Examples:
- vibrant;
- renowned;
- groundbreaking;
- world-class;
- cutting-edge;
- seamless.

**Edit:** specify the measurable feature, source the evaluation, or remove it.

### C. Generic analytical verbs

Examples:
- highlights;
- underscores;
- showcases;
- reflects;
- demonstrates.

**Edit:** identify who interprets the evidence that way or state the evidence without the interpretation.

### D. Canned transitions

Examples:
- additionally;
- moreover;
- furthermore;
- notably;
- in conclusion.

**Edit:** state the relationship directly.

### E. Empty completeness signals

Examples:
- comprehensive;
- holistic;
- multifaceted;
- diverse array;
- wide range.

**Edit:** enumerate what matters, if enumeration is useful.

### F. Vague authority

Examples:
- experts;
- observers;
- industry reports;
- studies show.

**Edit:** name the source.

This functional approach survives vocabulary changes better than a fixed blacklist because models, prompts, and fashionable words change.

## 27. Preserve useful imperfection, not accidental error

Human prose often contains asymmetry that should survive editing:

- one paragraph is one sentence because the point is simple;
- another paragraph is longer because the evidence is complex;
- a technical term repeats four times because replacing it would reduce precision;
- a sentence starts with "But" because the contrast is direct;
- a writer uses "I" in a reflective memo but not in the methods section;
- a parenthetical aside appears because the qualification is genuinely secondary.

An editor should not flatten all of these differences into the same polished template.

## 28. Research hierarchy for claims about AI writing

Not all evidence should receive equal weight.

### Higher confidence

- longitudinal corpora comparing language before and after widespread LLM use;
- controlled human-versus-LLM corpora;
- peer-reviewed studies with disclosed methods;
- established style manuals for punctuation and usage;
- actual pre-2022 writing when establishing historical baselines.

### Moderate confidence

- well-designed preprints with transparent datasets and limitations;
- model-specific experiments that may not generalize to newer systems;
- observations from one genre.

### Lower confidence

- screenshots of one chatbot answer;
- viral word lists;
- social-media claims that a punctuation mark "proves ChatGPT";
- detector scores without validation;
- anecdotes about a teacher or editor "always knowing";
- rules based on one author's taste.

The supplement gives the 2026 em-dash study useful but limited weight because it is a preprint, it concerns one political-writing corpus, and its author explicitly says the pattern is not a per-document detector.[S13]

## 29. Provenance is stronger than style guessing

Where authorship matters, better evidence includes:

- version history;
- tracked changes;
- research notes;
- source annotations;
- drafts;
- commit history;
- document metadata where appropriate and lawful;
- the author's ability to explain choices and sources.

A polished human text can look machine-written. A heavily edited AI draft can look human-written. Style alone cannot resolve every case.

## 30. Compact editorial standard for natural, credible prose

Before finalizing a document:

1. State facts before significance.
2. Prefer concrete nouns and exact verbs.
3. Keep ordinary words when they are precise.
4. Use the same technical term for the same thing.
5. Let sentence length follow thought length.
6. Remove automatic significance tails.
7. Verify every attribution.
8. Name sources instead of vague groups.
9. Use lists only when they improve scanning or comparison.
10. Use headings only when the document needs navigation.
11. Keep punctuation functional.
12. Use em dashes sparingly, but do not ban them.
13. Use hyphens according to compound-word grammar, not as decoration.
14. Do not force three-part structures.
15. Do not add a conclusion that merely repeats the introduction.
16. Do not fake personal experience.
17. Do not add deliberate errors.
18. Match the real genre and audience.
19. Preserve the author's stable language habits where they do not hurt clarity.
20. Read aloud, then make one final pass for evidence and specificity.

## 31. Examples of edits based on the combined research

### Example 1: significance inflation

**Before**
> The initiative marks a pivotal milestone in the company's ongoing journey toward innovation, highlighting its commitment to delivering robust and future-ready solutions.

**After**
> The company replaced its nightly batch import with an hourly pipeline and added automatic schema validation before records enter the warehouse.

**Why**
The revision removes self-congratulation and gives the reader observable changes.

### Example 2: vague authority

**Before**
> Industry experts have widely recognized the platform's growing importance.

**After**
> Gartner listed the product in its 2026 market guide for X, and the vendor reported 430 enterprise customers at the end of Q2.

**Why**
The revision separates a third-party mention from a vendor-reported number. Each claim can be checked.

### Example 3: participial significance tail

**Before**
> The museum opened a new archive in 2019, underscoring its enduring commitment to cultural preservation.

**After**
> The museum opened the archive in 2019. It holds 18,400 catalogued photographs dating from 1912 to 1987.

**Why**
The second sentence supplies information instead of interpretation.

### Example 4: decorative contrast

**Before**
> The redesign is not just visually appealing, but also enhances accessibility and user engagement.

**After**
> The redesign increased body text from 14 px to 16 px, added keyboard focus states, and removed the carousel from the home page.

**Why**
The revision reports the changes. If accessibility improved in testing, add the test result separately.

### Example 5: stacked compound adjectives

**Before**
> We provide a customer-centric, data-driven, AI-powered, future-ready marketing solution.

**After**
> The software combines campaign spend, lead, and CRM data in one dashboard and flags campaigns whose cost per qualified lead exceeds the team's threshold.

**Why**
The revision explains the product instead of branding it.

### Example 6: em-dash overuse

**Before**
> The service failed at 09:14—the cache cluster had reached its memory limit—and requests then fell back to the database—which increased latency across all three regions.

**After**
> The service failed at 09:14 because the cache cluster had reached its memory limit. Requests then fell back to the database, which increased latency across all three regions.

**Why**
The causal sequence is clearer as two sentences. The em dash was not grammatically wrong; it simply added more interruption than the explanation needed.

### Example 7: synonym rotation

**Before**
> The application stores the token in memory. The platform then sends the credential to the API. The system deletes the key after logout.

**After**
> The application stores the token in memory, sends the token to the API, and deletes the token after logout.

**Why**
If "token" is the correct technical term, repeating it is clearer than inventing three labels.

## 32. Notes on historical imitation

Reading old prose is useful for learning how many valid English styles exist. Copying an old writer's surface features is not a reliable route to natural modern writing.

Useful exercises include:

- rewrite a paragraph from memory after reading it, then compare choices;
- identify where an author uses concrete detail instead of abstraction;
- mark sentence lengths and ask why they change;
- track how pronouns and verbs carry agency;
- note where repetition is deliberate;
- observe punctuation in relation to syntax.

Avoid transplanting archaic diction, spelling, or sentence structures into modern business or technical writing merely to appear human.

The objective is not to sound old. It is to recover the habit of making sentence-level choices.

## 33. Final principle

The most durable distinction is not "AI words" versus "human words." It is **prefabricated language versus language chosen for a particular purpose**.

A strong draft is difficult to reduce to a generic template because its nouns, verbs, evidence, proportions, and structure are tied to the actual subject. That remains useful even as model-specific tells change.

## Research cautions

- Historical works demonstrate the range of human prose; they do not provide an authorship detector.
- Corpus-level associations should not be converted into rules about individual documents.
- Preprints [S11] and [S13] are useful current evidence but have not necessarily completed peer review.

- Genre, audience, language background, editing software, and house style can all create features that resemble alleged AI tells.
- The original Wikipedia guide remains the primary source for Wikipedia-specific signs such as wikitext errors, chatbot citation artifacts, and inappropriate editor-facing language.
